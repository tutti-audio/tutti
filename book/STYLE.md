# How the book is written

1. **Every program is real.** A program in the book is a file in
   `testing/tutti-book/examples/`, built and tested by CI, and the page shows
   it whole once, included by anchor
   (`{{#include ../../testing/tutti-book/examples/a_sound.rs:session}}` with
   `// ANCHOR: session` and `// ANCHOR_END: session` in the file).
2. **Small steps, intention first.** Before the whole program, a page walks
   through it a few lines at a time. Each step opens with what we want and
   why, in plain words and with no API name ("an empty studio is a quiet one,
   so let's add something that makes sound"); then one lead-in sentence; then
   the lines (`rust,ignore`, copied from the program, whose short comments
   carry the per-line detail); then a brief, conversational word on what we
   got. The prose reads as paragraphs, not as documentation of the code. No
   step shows more than a handful of lines. `tutti-book`'s `snippets` test
   checks that every such snippet is a run of lines of a program, so a
   snippet cannot drift. Lines a reader does not need are hidden with `# `.
3. **Every sound is on the page.** An example that makes sound renders it
   with tutti's own bounce to `book/src/sounds/<name>.flac`, and the page
   plays it with `<audio controls src="sounds/<name>.flac"></audio>` right
   after the code. "You should hear ..." says what to listen for. The sounds
   are generated by `cargo xtask book` and never committed.
4. **A page has one shape.** One sentence of what we build and why; the
   steps, one new idea each, naming its type as a link to its rustdoc and its
   building-block chapter; the whole program and the sound it makes; and
   something to try (change one number, hear the difference).
5. **Small before large.** Inside a building-block chapter, the smallest
   piece comes first (a node, a port, an edge), then what composes them, then
   the session call that does it for you. A term is defined before it is used.
6. **Voice.** Warm and friendly: we are a team guiding the reader through
   their first steps. "You" for the reader, "we" for the authors, the present
   tense, short plain sentences. Welcome newcomers and reassure them ("don't
   worry if ..."), celebrate a working result, and close a page by saying
   what comes next. A light touch of emoji (a 👋 to welcome, a 🎉 for a first
   sound) is fine; hype and marketing words are not. The words mean what they
   mean in the code: a *frame* is one value per channel, a *sample* is one
   value.
7. **Name the aliases.** Audio has many names for one thing. The first time
   a page uses a term, a short parenthesis gives the one or two others a
   reader is most likely to know: "the session's output (also known as the
   *master bus*, or *main out*)". The book's own word is the code's.
8. **Asides fold away.** Rust background goes in
   `<details><summary>🦀 ...</summary>`, audio background in
   `<details><summary>🎧 ...</summary>`. A rule that bites is a blockquote
   that opens with a bold sentence: "> **Never allocate on the audio
   thread.**"
9. **The book teaches; rustdoc is the reference.** A chapter links the items
   it uses and does not restate their docs.
